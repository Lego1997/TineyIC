# Howard Marks: Comprehensive Investment Philosophy Bibliography

## 1. Philosophy Distillation

Howard Marks, co-chairman and co-founder of Oaktree Capital Management (founded 1995, now managing ~$205 billion), represents a fundamentally distinct school within value investing: **risk-and-cycle-centric investing**. Where Buffett asks "Is this a great business?", Graham asks "Is this cheap enough?", and Lynch asks "Can this grow?", Marks asks: **"Where are we in the cycle, and is the risk being properly priced?"**

Marks's worldview rests on several interlocking convictions:

**First**, investing is fundamentally about dealing with the future, which is unknowable. As he writes: "The future is inherently uncertain, we usually have to choose between (a) avoiding risk and having little or no return, (b) taking a modest risk and settling for a commensurately modest return, or (c) taking on a high degree of uncertainty in pursuit of substantial gain but accepting the possibility of substantial permanent loss." Unlike academic finance, which treats risk as a quantifiable variable (beta, standard deviation), Marks treats risk as irreducibly subjective: "Here's the key to understanding risk: it's largely a matter of opinion."

**Second**, risk is NOT volatility -- risk is the probability of permanent capital loss. "I don't think most investors fear volatility... What they fear is the possibility of permanent loss." Investors "can ride out volatility but they never get a chance to undo a permanent loss." This is Marks's foundational disagreement with modern portfolio theory.

**Third**, risk is paradoxical and counterintuitive. Risk is highest precisely when everyone believes it is lowest: "A prime element in risk creation is a belief that risk is low, perhaps even gone altogether. That belief drives up prices and leads to the embrace of risky actions despite the lowness of prospective returns." Or more pithily: "The riskiest thing in the world is the belief that there's no risk." Prices rising makes assets appear safer but actually increases risk; prices falling makes assets appear riskier but actually decreases risk.

**Fourth**, markets are cyclical, driven by human psychology that swings like a pendulum between greed and fear. "Rule number one: most things will prove to be cyclical. Rule number two: some of the greatest opportunities for gain and loss come when other people forget rule number one." The investor's first job is not to forecast where the market is going, but to assess where it currently stands in the cycle.

**Fifth**, it is the PRICE you pay -- not the quality of the asset -- that determines your return. "Investment success doesn't come from 'buying good things,' but rather from 'buying things well.'" A great company at a terrible price is a terrible investment. A mediocre company at a bargain price can be a great one. "It is price, not quality, that determines value."

**Sixth**, superior returns require second-level thinking -- thinking that is not merely correct but also non-consensus. "You can't do the same things others do and expect to outperform." "Extraordinary performance comes only from correct nonconsensus forecasts." The market is a voting machine in the short run; merely being right is not enough -- you must be right in a way that differs from the crowd.

**Seventh**, the goal is asymmetric returns: participating more in gains than in losses. "If we avoid the losers, the winners will take care of themselves." This is Oaktree's motto. Defense -- avoiding catastrophic mistakes -- is more important than offense. "We have to practice defensive investing, since many of the outcomes are likely to go against us. It's more important to ensure survival under negative outcomes than it is to guarantee maximum returns under favorable ones."

This philosophy was forged in the credit markets, not equities. Marks began his career in 1969 at Citicorp, managed high-yield and convertible securities from 1978, and credits a 1978 meeting with Michael Milken with crystallizing his core insight: "It's not what you buy, but what you pay for it." At TCW Group (1985-1995) and then Oaktree (1995-present), he specialized in distressed debt and high-yield bonds -- markets where the asymmetry between upside and downside is the entire game, where credit analysis and risk assessment matter more than growth projections.

Warren Buffett has said of Marks's memos: "When I see memos from Howard Marks in my mail, they're the first thing I open and read. I always learn something." The memos, written since October 1990, now number approximately 160 and have been deposited in the permanent collection of the Museum of American Finance.

## 2. Mental Models Catalog

### 2.1 Second-Level Thinking

**Definition**: "Your thinking has to be better than that of others -- both more powerful and at a higher level." First-level thinking is "simplistic and superficial, and just about everyone can do it." Second-level thinking is "deep, complex, and convoluted."

**The fundamental insight**: "Being right may be a necessary condition for investment success, but it won't be sufficient." You must be right AND different from consensus, because consensus expectations are already embedded in prices.

**Marks's canonical examples of first-level vs. second-level thinking:**

| First-Level Thinking | Second-Level Thinking |
|---|---|
| "It's a good company; let's buy the stock." | "It's a good company, but everyone thinks it's a great company, and it's not. So the stock's overrated and overpriced; let's sell." |
| "The outlook calls for low growth and rising inflation. Let's dump our stocks." | "The outlook stinks, but everyone else is selling in panic. Buy!" |
| "I think the company's earnings will fall; sell." | "I think the company's earnings will fall less than people expect, and the pleasant surprise will lift the stock; buy." |

**Additional questions second-level thinkers ask**: "How does the current price for the asset comport with the consensus view of the future and with mine? Is the consensus psychology that's incorporated in the price too bullish or bearish?"

**The key test**: "Who doesn't know that?" If information is widely known, it is already in the price: "If you and everyone else know something, it's already discounted in the price of the stock."

**Source**: "The Most Important Thing" Chapter 1; "It's Not Easy" memo (September 2015); "I Beg to Differ" memo.

### 2.2 The Pendulum

**Core formulation**: "The mood swings of the securities markets resemble the movement of a pendulum." Markets spend very little time at the midpoint (fair value) and most time swinging toward extremes.

"One of the most time-honored market adages says that 'markets fluctuate between greed and fear.' There's a fundamental reason for this: it's because people fluctuate between greed and fear."

**The pendulum swings between**:
- Greed and fear
- Euphoria and depression
- Risk tolerance and risk aversion
- "No price too high" and "sale at any price"
- Credulity and skepticism
- Faith in the future and concern about the future

**Operational use**: Marks does not try to predict where the pendulum will go next. Instead, he observes where it currently is. When the pendulum is at one extreme, the overwhelming probability is a swing back toward the other extreme -- not a further push in the same direction.

**Three stages of a bull market** (attributed to market lore, frequently cited by Marks):
1. When a few forward-looking people begin to believe things will get better
2. When most investors realize improvement is actually underway
3. When everyone concludes things will get better forever

**Three stages of a bear market**: The reverse.

**Source**: "The Most Important Thing" Chapter 9; "Mastering the Market Cycle" extensively.

### 2.3 "Where Are We in the Cycle?"

This is Marks's FIRST and most important question. Not "Where is the market going?" but "Where are we NOW?"

"We may never know where we're going, but we ought to know where we are."

"One thing I believe in most strongly is the inevitability of cycles. I always say that while we can't know where we're going, we ought to know where we are."

**Marks's 7-point framework for "Taking the Temperature"** (from "Taking the Temperature" memo, July 2023):

1. **Pattern recognition**: Study historical market cycles to understand today's implications. "Investor psychology and thus market cycles fluctuate in ways that approach dependability."
2. **Understanding excesses and corrections**: "A strong movement in one direction is more likely to be followed by a correction in the opposite direction" than by endless continuation.
3. **Monitor emotional extremes**: Watch for moments when investors become excessively optimistic or deeply pessimistic.
4. **Practice informed contrarianism**: "Successful contrarianism requires understanding what the herd does, why, what's wrong with it, and what should happen instead."
5. **Track emotional swings**: "Much of what happens in economies and markets results from the emotional swings of investors."
6. **Control your own emotions**: "Resist joining crowd psychology. Stand apart."
7. **Identify illogical propositions**: "Spot widely accepted statements that don't make logical sense or seem 'too good/bad to be true.'"

**Specific indicators Marks watches** (from "It's All Good" memo, July 2007):
- Level of skepticism, fear, and risk aversion in the market
- Willingness to undertake risky investments
- How easily capital can be accessed
- Whether promised returns from safe investments seem "so meager" that investors reach for yield
- Level of leverage being employed
- Quality of securitization structures being sold

**Source**: "Taking the Temperature" memo (July 2023); "It's All Good" memo (July 2007); "Mastering the Market Cycle" (2018 book).

### 2.4 Risk Is Not Volatility

**Marks's fundamental disagreement with academic finance**: Modern portfolio theory (CAPM, efficient markets) defines risk as volatility (standard deviation of returns, beta). Marks categorically rejects this.

"Risk means more things can happen than will happen." Risk is best understood as a probability distribution of outcomes -- not a single number.

"The possibility of permanent loss is the risk I worry about."

**The measurement problem**: Risk cannot be assessed after the fact. Using a weather analogy, Marks argues that what happens "tomorrow will tell you what the probability of rain was as of today" -- nothing. Just because an investment worked out doesn't mean it wasn't risky. Just because it didn't work out doesn't mean it was riskier than thought. "Only one thing will happen" -- but risk was about all the things that could have happened.

**The paradox**: "Risk is counterintuitive -- prices declining increase actual risk while seeming riskier to markets; rising prices decrease actual risk while appearing safer." In plain language: the market thinks cheap assets are risky and expensive assets are safe; Marks believes exactly the opposite.

**24 types of risk** (cataloged in "Risk Revisited Again" memo): credit risk, illiquidity risk, concentration risk, leverage risk, funding risk, manager risk, basis risk, model risk, black swan risk, fundamental risk, valuation risk, interest rate risk, purchasing power risk, and others.

**Source**: "Risk Revisited" memo (September 2014); "Risk Revisited Again"; "The Most Important Thing" Chapters 5-7; "The Indispensability of Risk" memo (April 2024).

### 2.5 Asymmetric Risk/Return (Oaktree's Core)

The goal of investing is not merely to make money -- it is to achieve asymmetry: "To expose yourself to return in a way that doesn't expose you commensurately to risk, and to participate in gains when the market rises to a greater extent than you participate in losses when it falls."

"If we avoid the losers, the winners will take care of themselves." -- Oaktree's founding principle.

**Offensive vs. Defensive investing**: "Rather than doing the right thing, the defensive investor's main emphasis is on not doing the wrong thing." Defense "can be seen as an attempt at higher returns, but more through the avoidance of minuses than through the inclusion of pluses, and more through consistent but perhaps moderate progress than through occasional flashes of brilliance."

**Oaktree's performance goal**: "Oaktree portfolios are set up to outperform in bad times, and if they can keep up in good times and outperform in bad times, they'll have above-average results over full cycles with below-average volatility."

**Source**: Oaktree Investment Philosophy page; "Fewer Losers, or More Winners?" memo (September 2023); "The Most Important Thing" Chapters 17-19.

### 2.6 The Relationship Between Price and Value

"An accurate estimate of intrinsic value is the indispensable starting point."

"No asset is too good if overpaid; few are too bad if cheap enough." "Well bought is half sold."

"Buying something for less than its value -- this is what it's all about."

"High-quality assets can be risky, and low-quality assets can be safe." It all depends on the price.

"Investing is a popularity contest, and the most dangerous thing is to buy something at the peak of its popularity. At that point, all favorable facts and opinions are already factored into its price, and no new buyers are left to emerge."

"High prices imply high risk, and low prices imply low risk."

**Source**: "The Most Important Thing" Chapters 3-4; numerous memos.

### 2.7 Knowing What You Don't Know

"I believe absolutely that there is no place in our profession for certainty." Words like "always," "never," "has to," and "can't" signal dangerous overconfidence.

"I think it's very important for your success to be brutally frank about what you don't know."

"The key prerequisites for risk control are humility, lack of hubris, and knowing what you don't know."

Marks distinguishes between the **knowable** (company fundamentals, credit quality, asset value) and the **unknowable** (macro trends, interest rate direction, geopolitical events). He invests based on the former and refuses to bet on the latter.

This reflects Warren Buffett's formulation that Marks frequently cites: "For a piece of information to be desirable, it has to satisfy two criteria: it has to be important, and it has to be knowable." The macro is extremely important -- but it's not knowable.

**Source**: "The Most Important Thing" Chapter 14; "You Can't Predict. You Can Prepare" memo (November 2001); Money Maze Podcast interview.

### 2.8 Anti-Forecasting

"We may never know where we're going, but we ought to know where we are."

Marks holds a "limited opinion of macro forecasts" and is "convinced that they aren't consistently actionable and valuable." Economic forecasts are inherently unreliable because "future events cannot be predicted with any consistency" and "economics lacks the precision of physics because human behavior remains inherently unpredictable."

**Oaktree's Fifth Tenet**: "Macro-forecasting is not critical to investing." Investment decisions are based on bottom-up, company-specific research, not economic predictions.

"Preparing really means not preparing for one outcome, but for having a portfolio which prepares you for a range of outcomes."

**Source**: "You Can't Predict. You Can Prepare" memo (November 2001); Oaktree Investment Philosophy; "The Most Important Thing" Chapter 14.

### 2.9 Patient Opportunism

"Patient opportunism -- waiting for bargains -- is often your best strategy."

"Patient opportunism, buttressed by a contrarian attitude and a strong balance sheet, can yield amazing profits during meltdowns."

**Prerequisites for patient opportunism**: (1) Reliance on assessed value, (2) minimal or no leverage, (3) long-term committed capital, (4) strong psychological discipline during crises, (5) willingness to forgo fees/returns while waiting.

**2008 example**: Oaktree raised an $11 billion distressed debt fund and waited a full year before deploying capital, forgoing approximately $160 million in annual management fees. Over a three-month stretch during the crisis, they invested $6 billion in distressed assets, which generated $6 billion in gains for investors and $1.5 billion for the partners.

**Source**: "The Most Important Thing" Chapter 13; "The Limits to Negativism" memo (October 2008).

### 2.10 Contrarianism (Non-Consensus + Correct)

"Large amounts of money aren't made by buying what everybody likes. They're made by buying what everybody underestimates."

**Marks's crucial refinement**: Being contrarian is necessary but NOT sufficient. "You must deviate from the herd AND be more correct." Simply disagreeing with the crowd gets you nowhere if you're wrong.

"Non-consensus ideas have to be lonely. By definition, non-consensus ideas that are popular, widely held or intuitively obvious are an oxymoron. Thus such ideas are uncomfortable; non-conformists don't enjoy the warmth that comes with being at the center of the herd."

**David Swensen (quoted by Marks)**: "Superior investing requires 'uncomfortably idiosyncratic behavior'... If you're not a little uncomfortable, there's something wrong."

**Source**: "Dare to Be Great" memo (September 2006); "I Beg to Differ" memo; "The Most Important Thing" Chapter 11.

## 3. Second-Level Thinking: Complete Formulation with Examples

### The Complete Framework

**Step 1 -- Surface analysis (first-level)**: What does the data say? Is this a good company? Are earnings rising or falling?

**Step 2 -- Consensus assessment**: What does the MARKET think? What expectations are already embedded in the price?

**Step 3 -- Differential insight**: How does MY view differ from consensus? Am I seeing something others miss?

**Step 4 -- Price check**: Given the consensus view and my differential view, is this asset priced to deliver adequate returns?

**Step 5 -- Risk assessment**: What are the ways I could be wrong? What is the range of outcomes?

### Extended Examples

**Example 1 -- The "Good Company" Trap**:
- First-level: "Apple is a great company. Buy."
- Second-level: "Apple is a great company, but everyone thinks it's great and the stock is priced for perfection. Any disappointment in earnings, even a minor one, will cause a sharp decline. The risk/reward is unfavorable at this price."

**Example 2 -- The Panic Sell**:
- First-level: "The economy is heading into recession. Sell everything."
- Second-level: "The economy is heading into recession, but everyone knows it and stocks are already pricing in a severe downturn. The recession may be milder than feared. At these prices, the risk of NOT owning stocks exceeds the risk of owning them."

**Example 3 -- The Earnings Miss**:
- First-level: "This company missed earnings. Sell."
- Second-level: "This company missed earnings, but the miss was smaller than the market feared. The stock has been beaten down too far. Buy on the positive surprise relative to expectations."

**Example 4 -- The Hot Sector**:
- First-level: "AI is going to transform the world. Buy AI stocks."
- Second-level: "AI will transform the world, but that knowledge is universal and priced in. Many AI companies trade at prices that assume near-perfect execution for a decade. The question isn't whether AI wins, but whether the current prices adequately compensate for the risk that some of these companies won't be the winners."

**Example 5 -- The Distressed Asset**:
- First-level: "This bond is rated junk. Too risky."
- Second-level: "This bond is rated junk, which is why it yields 15%. If the company can service its debt -- which my analysis says it can -- the reward vastly exceeds the risk. The market's revulsion at the rating is my opportunity."

**Example 6 -- The Housing Bubble (Marks's own example)**:
- First-level (2006): "Housing prices have always gone up. Mortgage-backed securities are safe."
- Second-level: "The belief that housing prices never fall is historically unprecedented and therefore probably wrong. The volume of subprime lending suggests the system is fragile. When prices stop rising, the entire structure unwinds."

## 4. The Pendulum and Cycle Framework: Full Operational Description

### The Nature of Cycles

Marks views cycles as the single most important structural feature of markets. In "Mastering the Market Cycle" (2018), he argues that understanding cycles does not require prediction -- it requires recognition.

**The credit cycle is the most important cycle** because it directly affects the availability and cost of capital:
1. Economy improves -> lenders become optimistic -> lending standards decline
2. Capital becomes easy to access -> borrowers take on more risk -> asset prices rise
3. Rising prices confirm optimism -> risk is perceived as low -> leverage increases
4. Eventually, some loans go bad -> lenders tighten -> capital becomes scarce
5. Asset prices fall -> more loans go bad -> panic sets in -> prices overshoot to the downside
6. At the bottom, fear prevents lending -> assets become deeply undervalued -> the cycle resets

**Key Marks quotes on cycles**:
- "No matter how favorable and steady fundamentals may be, the markets will always be subject to substantial cyclical fluctuation."
- "Good times teach only bad lessons."
- "Busts are the product of booms."
- "The herd applies optimism at the top and pessimism at the bottom."
- "Stocks are cheapest when everything looks grim."

### Operational Application

**When the pendulum is at the euphoria extreme** (indicators: low spreads, easy credit, minimal fear, leverage rising, IPO mania, "no price too high"):
- Move toward defensiveness
- Reduce exposure to risk assets
- Accept lower returns in exchange for protection
- Raise cash or quality
- "When other people are carefree, we should be worried"

**When the pendulum is at the fear extreme** (indicators: wide spreads, tight credit, universal pessimism, forced selling, "wouldn't touch it at any price"):
- Move toward aggressiveness
- Deploy capital into distressed/dislocated assets
- Accept short-term pain for long-term gain
- "When other people are terrified, we can turn aggressive"
- "The negatives are on the table, optimism is thoroughly lacking, and the greater long-term risk probably lies in not investing"

**The midpoint** (where the pendulum spends the least time):
- Maintain standard portfolio positioning
- Neither notably aggressive nor notably defensive
- Focus on bottom-up value

### Marks's Signal Checklist for Cycle Position

From various memos, Marks evaluates:
- Credit spreads (tight = euphoria, wide = fear)
- Lending standards (loose = danger, tight = opportunity)
- Investor risk tolerance (high = be cautious, low = be aggressive)
- Leverage levels in the system
- Quality of new issuance (weaker deals getting funded = late cycle)
- Investor willingness to accept low returns
- Volume of "too good to be true" propositions
- Level of media/public enthusiasm or fear
- Whether "this time it's different" narratives are prevalent

## 5. Step-by-Step Decision Process with Source Quotes

### Step 1: Assess Market Position in the Cycle

**Question**: "Where are we in the cycle?"

**Quote**: "Where do we stand in the cycle? In my opinion, there's little mystery. I see low levels of skepticism, fear, and risk aversion. Most people are willing to undertake risky investments, often because the promised returns from traditional, safe investments seem so meager." -- "It's All Good" memo, July 2007

**Action**: Determine whether the environment calls for offense or defense.

### Step 2: Apply Second-Level Thinking

**Question**: What does the consensus expect, and what's already priced in?

**Quote**: "The first-level thinker simply looks for the highest-quality company, the best product, the fastest earnings growth, or the lowest p/e ratio. He's ignorant of the very existence of a second level at which to think." -- "It's Not Easy" memo, 2015

**Action**: Identify where your view diverges from consensus and assess whether the price reflects consensus or your view.

### Step 3: Evaluate the Asymmetry

**Question**: What is the potential upside vs. downside?

**Quote**: "The goal to investing is asymmetry: to expose yourself to return in a way that doesn't expose you commensurately to risk." -- "Fewer Losers, or More Winners?" memo, 2023

**Action**: Estimate the range of outcomes. Look for situations where the downside is limited (price already discounts bad news) and the upside is significant (if things aren't as bad as feared).

### Step 4: Assess Risk (Probability of Permanent Loss)

**Question**: What is the probability that I permanently lose capital here?

**Quote**: "Investment risk resides most where it is least perceived." "There are few things as risky as the widespread belief that there's no risk." -- "The Most Important Thing Illuminated"

**Action**: Focus on loss probability, not volatility. Ask: What are the scenarios in which this investment goes to zero or near-zero? How likely are they? Is the price compensating me for this risk?

### Step 5: Only Invest When Risk Is Being Mispriced

**Question**: Is the market pricing too much or too little risk?

**Quote**: "When everyone believes something embodies no risk, they usually bid it up to the point where it's enormously risky." -- "The Most Important Thing" Chapter 6

**Quote**: "If the negative event did come to pass, the price would change little... But, if the negative event did not come to pass, then the asset would increase dramatically." -- "The Limits to Negativism" memo, October 2008

**Action**: In euphoria, the market prices too little risk (prices too high, spreads too tight). In panic, the market prices too much risk (prices too low, spreads too wide). Invest primarily when risk is being overpriced by the market.

### Step 6: Be Patient -- It's OK to Do Nothing

**Question**: Do I need to act now, or should I wait?

**Quote**: "Patient opportunism -- waiting for bargains -- is often your best strategy." -- "The Most Important Thing" Chapter 13

**Quote**: "Most people say everything's either a buy or a sell. I don't think that's true. I think there are things that are legitimate holds." -- Money Maze Podcast interview

**Action**: Resist the urge to always be doing something. "The best opportunities are usually found among things most others won't do." Wait for the asymmetry to be strongly in your favor.

### Communication Style

Marks writes in a **philosophical, essay-like, pedagogical style** that is distinctive among investment writers:

- **Builds arguments through Socratic questioning**: He poses the question, examines multiple sides, then reaches a nuanced conclusion. Never gives simple buy/sell recommendations.
- **Uses analogies from sports, poker, backgammon, chess**: Every decade he writes a memo connecting games/sports to investing. "Behind the Memo: The Indispensability of Risk" was inspired by chess grandmaster Maurice Ashley.
- **Cross-disciplinary connections**: Draws from philosophy, psychology, history, literature. He sees connections "among ostensibly unconnected things."
- **Admires and emulates Buffett's metaphorical style**: Strives for "pithy, visually-evocative metaphors." The airport moving walkway (for declining interest rates) is a prime example.
- **Always nuanced, never definitive**: "I believe absolutely that there is no place in our profession for certainty." His language carefully avoids absolutes.
- **Measured, reflective prose**: Different from Graham's dry academic rigor, Buffett's folksy humor, Lynch's enthusiastic storytelling, and Munger's acerbic wit.
- **Writing process**: Accumulates "a pile of scraps of paper, a list of topics and thoughts" until the pile becomes substantial, then writes the memo. No formal outline.
- Values feedback that says "You make complex things simple."

## 7. Contrastive Beliefs

### What Marks Explicitly Rejects

1. **Risk = Volatility**: His foundational rejection of modern portfolio theory. Standard deviation and beta do not measure what investors actually fear.
2. **Macro forecasting as actionable**: "Forecasts are unlikely to help us foresee the movements of the economic cycle." If you spend time trying to predict GDP or interest rates, you're wasting it.
3. **Simple buy/sell without risk context**: Everything must be framed in terms of risk/reward at the current price and current cycle position.
4. **The obligation to always be fully invested**: "Waiting for bargains is often your best strategy." Unlike many fund managers who feel pressure to deploy capital, Marks believes doing nothing is a valid strategy.
5. **Ignoring cycles**: Many investors -- including buy-and-hold purists -- act as though cycles don't exist. Marks considers this dangerous.
6. **Certainty in any form**: Avoids words like "always," "never," "has to," "can't."
7. **The idea that higher quality = lower risk**: Risk is a function of PRICE, not quality. A high-quality asset at an excessive price is riskier than a low-quality asset at a bargain price.
8. **Formulas for investing success**: "Anyone who thinks there's a formula for investing that guarantees success clearly doesn't understand the complex, dynamic, and competitive nature of the investing process."
9. **The EMH in its strong form**: Markets are often wrong. But beating the market consistently requires an edge -- and most people don't have one.

### How Marks Differs from Other Investors

#### Marks vs. Warren Buffett

| Dimension | Marks | Buffett |
|-----------|-------|---------|
| **Primary focus** | Risk assessment and cycle position | Business quality and competitive moats |
| **Cycle awareness** | Central -- actively adjusts offense/defense based on cycle position | Largely cycle-agnostic -- "Our favorite holding period is forever" |
| **Asset class** | Credit/distressed debt primarily | Equities and whole businesses |
| **Diversification** | Broad diversification within niches | Concentrated portfolio |
| **Selling** | Will sell when cycle position warrants | Rarely sells a great business |
| **Timing** | Timing (within a cycle-aware framework) matters | Timing matters little if the business is great |
| **Primary goal** | Limit downside, let upside take care of itself | Compound at high returns over decades |
| **Communication** | Philosophical essays with nuance | Folksy storytelling with wit |
| **Overlap** | Both agree: price matters, market is often irrational, patience is essential. Buffett reads Marks's memos first. |

| Dimension | Marks | Graham |
|-----------|-------|--------|

## 8. Risk Framework (CORE SECTION)

### 8.1 The Definition of Risk

"To me, risk is the most interesting, challenging and essential aspect of investing."

Risk, for Marks, is **the probability distribution of possible outcomes**, particularly the probability of permanent capital loss.

"Risk means more things can happen than will happen." This means we must think of the future "not in terms of a single result but rather as a range of possibilities, with a probability distribution summarizing the possibilities and describing their relative likelihood."

Risk is NOT:
- Volatility (standard deviation)
- Beta
- The certainty of loss
- A function of asset quality

Risk IS:
- The probability of permanent capital loss
- Subjective: "Here's the key to understanding risk: it's largely a matter of opinion"
- Unknowable in advance and unmeasurable even after the fact
- A function of PRICE relative to value

### 8.2 The Perversity of Risk

This is Marks's most counterintuitive and important insight:

**Risk increases as prices rise and decreases as prices fall.** This is the exact opposite of how most investors feel.

"When everyone believes something embodies no risk, they usually bid it up to the point where it's enormously risky."

"A prime element in risk creation is a belief that risk is low, perhaps even gone altogether."

"Risk is high when investors feel risk is low. And risk compensation is at a minimum just when risk is at a maximum (meaning risk compensation is most needed)."

"Investment risk resides most where it is least perceived."

The mechanism: When investors feel safe, they pay higher prices, accept lower spreads, use more leverage, and accept weaker covenants. Each of these actions INCREASES actual risk while making investors FEEL less risky.

Conversely: When investors are terrified, they sell at any price, demand wide spreads, refuse to lend, and insist on strong protections. Each of these actions DECREASES actual risk while making investors FEEL most at risk.

### 8.3 Risk Cannot Be Measured After the Fact

"Risk cannot be assessed after an investment rises or falls." Outcomes reveal outcomes -- they do not reveal the risk that existed at the time of the decision.

The weather analogy: Saying it didn't rain doesn't tell you the probability of rain was low. It just means one outcome materialized. The decision to carry an umbrella might have been perfectly rational even if it didn't rain.

"Future events cannot be predicted with any consistency. Economics lacks the precision of physics because human behavior remains inherently unpredictable."

### 8.4 Risk and Return: The Real Relationship

Academic finance teaches: higher risk = higher return. Marks corrects this: "Investments that seem riskier have to APPEAR LIKELY to deliver higher returns, or else people won't make them." Risk doesn't guarantee returns -- it merely enables them.

"You shouldn't expect to make money without bearing risk, but you shouldn't expect to make money just for taking risk."

"Riskier investments needn't deliver higher returns -- if they reliably did, they wouldn't be riskier."

A better formulation: Higher-risk investments have a WIDER range of possible outcomes. The expected return may be higher, but the range includes both much better AND much worse outcomes than safer investments.

### 8.5 Defensive vs. Aggressive Investing

"You can't simultaneously emphasize both preservation of capital and maximization of growth."

**Defensive investing** (Marks's default):
- Prioritizes avoiding losses over achieving gains
- "Invest scared, requiring good value and a substantial margin for error"
- "Being conscious of what you don't know and can't control are hallmarks of the best investors I know"
- Favors consistency over brilliance

**Aggressive investing** (appropriate at cycle lows):
- Appropriate when fear is extreme and prices are deeply depressed
- Must be supported by analysis, not just contrarian instinct
- "When other people are terrified, we can turn aggressive"
- Time-limited -- return to defense as the cycle progresses

**Oaktree's approach**: "Risk control number one, consistency number two." Target: above-average results over full cycles with below-average volatility. "It's easy to make money in the market... The challenge, the real skill is to make money with the risk under control so that if it turns out to be a bad year instead you won't do too badly."

### 8.6 The Indispensability of Risk (2024 Memo Framework)

Marks's refinement: Avoiding all risk is itself the greatest risk.

"There's such a thing as the risk of taking too little risk."

"Risk avoidance is likely to result in return avoidance."

"Not being willing to take risks is an extremely risky strategy." (Magnus Carlsen, chess, quoted by Marks)

"Investors must accept that success is likely to stem from making a large number of investments, all of which you make because you expect them to succeed, but some portion of which you know won't."

The chess analogy: In chess, "many positions cannot be won or saved without something of value being given away." Two types of sacrifices: "sham" sacrifices yield immediate returns; "real" sacrifices offer gains that are "neither immediate nor tangible." Great investing, like great chess, requires the willingness to make real sacrifices.

### 8.7 Credit Analysis: The Oaktree Lens

Marks's background in high-yield bonds and distressed debt gives his risk framework a distinctive credit-market flavor:

- 17 distressed debt funds have generated up to 19% annual returns (net of fees)
- Bruce Karsh's distressed team returned 18% net for 23+ years without leverage
- Capitalized on dislocations during Asian Financial Crisis (1998), dot-com bust (2001), and Global Financial Crisis (2008)

**Key credit principles**:
- "The worst of loans are made in the best of times"
- Yield spreads exist because sub-investment grade debt carries REAL credit risk
- Superior credit analysis requires assembling a "mosaic" of information, not finding single smoking guns
- "Risk is my friend. The more risk I take, the more money I make" -- the dangerous thinking that emerges at cycle peaks

### Oaktree's Six Investment Tenets (Unchanged Since 1995)

1. **The Primacy of Risk Control**: "If we avoid the losers, the winners will take care of themselves."
2. **Emphasis on Consistency**: Above-average results through consistent, high-batting-average performance rather than volatile brilliance.
3. **The Importance of Market Inefficiency**: Invest only in less efficient markets where skill creates edge.
4. **The Benefits of Specialization**: "You can't be a successful generalist in my opinion. You have to know more than everybody else about a few things."
5. **Macro-Forecasting Is Not Critical**: Bottom-up, company-specific research over top-down economic predictions.
6. **Disavowal of Market Timing**: Stay fully invested in attractively priced assets; don't raise cash based on macro fears. (Note the nuance: Marks adjusts offense/defense based on cycle position, but doesn't go to cash.)

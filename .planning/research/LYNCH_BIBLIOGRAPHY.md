# Peter Lynch: Comprehensive Investment Philosophy Bibliography

## 1. Philosophy Distillation

Peter Lynch is the architect of **Growth at a Reasonable Price (GARP)** — a hybrid philosophy that marries the growth investor's hunger for rapidly expanding earnings with the value investor's insistence on paying a sensible price. He managed the Fidelity Magellan Fund from May 1977 to May 1990, growing it from $18 million to over $14 billion in assets, averaging a 29.2% annual return over 13 years. The fund underperformed the S&P 500 only twice during his tenure. An initial $1,000 investment in Magellan in 1977 would have grown to approximately $28,000 by 1990.

Lynch's philosophy rests on several interlocking pillars:

**The Amateur's Edge.** Lynch believed — and proved — that individual investors have a structural advantage over institutional money managers. Institutions cannot invest in small companies due to trading volume constraints and risk mandates. The individual, unburdened by these constraints, can spot opportunities in everyday life before Wall Street catches on.

> "Your investor's edge is not something you get from Wall Street experts. It's something you already have."
> — *One Up on Wall Street* (1989)

> "Average investors can become experts in their own field and can pick winning stocks as effectively as Wall Street professionals."
> — *One Up on Wall Street*

**Earnings Are the North Star.** Lynch's framework begins and ends with earnings. Every other metric — P/E, PEG, balance sheet ratios — is a lens for understanding the earnings trajectory.

> "I don't think people understand there's 100% correlation with what happens to a company's earnings over several years and what happens to the stock."
> — Charlie Rose interview, 1993

> "If you can follow only one bit of data, follow the earnings."
> — *One Up on Wall Street*

> "Often, there is no correlation between the success of a company's operations and the success of its stock over a few months or a few years. In the long term, there is 100 percent correlation between the success of the company and the success of its stock."
> — *One Up on Wall Street*

**Classification Before Analysis.** Unlike Buffett, who seeks one archetype (wonderful companies at fair prices), Lynch insists that different types of companies demand fundamentally different evaluation frameworks. His six-category taxonomy (detailed in Section 2) is the first analytical move.

**Story-Driven Conviction.** Every stock Lynch owned had a "story" — a simple, understandable narrative about why the company would do well. If you couldn't articulate that story in two minutes, you didn't understand the investment.

> "If you're prepared to invest in a company, then you ought to be able to explain why in simple language that a fifth grader could understand."
> — *One Up on Wall Street*

**What makes GARP different from pure value investing:** Graham and traditional value investors focus on buying assets below intrinsic value using quantitative screens — low P/B, low P/E, high net current asset values. Lynch actively *hunts for growth* and is willing to pay up for it, provided the price is reasonable relative to the growth rate (PEG < 1). He embraces qualitative judgment, consumer observation, and forward-looking earnings estimates — domains that Graham's statistical, backward-looking approach explicitly avoids. Lynch also holds a shorter time horizon (1-5 years for stalwarts, 3-10 years for fast growers) compared to Buffett's "forever" holding period.

> "Investing in stocks is an art, not a science, and people trained to rigidly quantify everything have a big disadvantage."
> — *One Up on Wall Street*

> "Studying history and philosophy was much better preparation for the stock market than studying statistics."
> — *One Up on Wall Street*

## 2. Complete Six Stock Categories Taxonomy

Lynch's taxonomy appears primarily in **Chapters 7-8 of *One Up on Wall Street*** ("I've Got It, I've Got It — What Is It?" and "The Perfect Stock, What a Deal!"). The selling criteria are found in **Chapter 17** ("The Best Time to Buy and Sell"). The Final Checklist is **Chapter 15**.

### 2.1 Slow Growers

**Definition:** Large, aging companies that have matured past their growth phase. Typically former fast growers that saturated their markets. Growth rate slightly above GDP (2-4% annually).

**Characteristics:**
- Pay generous, regular dividends (they have run out of new ways to deploy capital)
- Flat earnings trajectory
- Often former fast growers that have gone through their growth cycle (Lynch uses the example of electric utilities)

**Evaluation Criteria:**
- Dividend yield: Is it attractive and sustainable?
- Dividend payment history: Has it been increased consistently?
- Payout ratio: Lower ratios provide a cushion during downturns; higher ratios carry greater risk of dividend cuts
- Dividend safety: Does the company have enough cash and revenue to sustain increasing dividends?

**Buy Signal:** Attractive dividend yield with low payout ratio and consistent history of increases.

**Sell Signal:** When dividends and buybacks attract a different kind of investor who loves the "apparent safety" — this often signals the stock is fully valued for its category.

**Lynch's Verdict on Slow Growers:** He was generally NOT interested. He preferred to find companies still in their growth phase. Slow growers sit in the portfolio only for income and stability.

### 2.2 Stalwarts

**Definition:** Large, established companies growing faster than slow growers but not as aggressively as fast growers. Typically 10-12% annual earnings growth. Think Coca-Cola, Procter & Gamble, Bristol-Myers.

**Characteristics:**
- Multi-billion dollar companies that still grow reliably
- Unlikely to go bankrupt
- Excellent recession protection
- Dependable but not spectacular returns

**Evaluation Criteria:**
- P/E ratio relative to historical range and industry peers
- Check for "diworseification" — acquisitions outside core competence that dilute earnings quality
- Long-term growth momentum consistency
- Performance during previous recessions and market downturns
- Stock buyback activity (positive signal)

**Buy Signal:** P/E at the low end of its historical range; stock hasn't gone anywhere for two years but company has improved itself.

**Sell Signal:** Make 30-50% and sell. Rotate into another undervalued stalwart. Lynch expects "good but not enormous returns — certainly no more than 50% in two years."

**Selling Red Flags:**
- P/E strays too far above its normal historical range
- New products have poor results
- Company is "diworseifying" via unrelated acquisitions

**Two-Minute Drill Example (Coca-Cola):** "Coca-Cola is selling at the low end of its P/E range. The stock hasn't gone anywhere for two years. The company has improved itself in several ways..."

**Portfolio Role:** Lynch keeps stalwarts as recession insurance. During market crashes, their P/E drops but recovers quickly as the market recovers.

### 2.3 Fast Growers

**Definition:** Small, aggressive enterprises growing earnings at 20-25% annually. Lynch's FAVORITE category and the "land of the ten-baggers."

> "These are among my favorite investments: small aggressive new enterprises that grow at 20 to 25 percent a year. If you choose wisely, this is the land of the 10- to 40-baggers, and even the 200-baggers."
> — *One Up on Wall Street*, Chapter 7

**Characteristics:**
- 20-25% annual earnings growth (Lynch cautions against 50%+ growth as unsustainable)
- Small to mid-cap, often underfollowed
- Proven concept being replicated across locations/markets
- Need not be in a fast-growing industry (Lynch prefers fast growers in slow-growth industries)

**Evaluation Criteria:**
- Verify the key product represents a significant portion of revenue (not a side project)
- Earnings growth rate: Target 20-25% annually; be suspicious of 50-100% growth
- PEG ratio: P/E should be at or below the growth rate (PEG <= 1)
- Proven multi-location expansion success (e.g., successful rollout from one city to multiple cities)
- Remaining growth runway — how much room is left to expand?
- Measure expansion acceleration vs. deceleration
- Low institutional ownership and analyst coverage (positive signal)
- Good balance sheets with substantial profits
- Insider buying activity

**Buy Signal:** PEG < 1, low institutional ownership, proven expansion model with long remaining runway, earnings accelerating.

**Sell Triggers:**
- Growth is slowing — the market penalizes fast growers immediately when growth decelerates
- 40+ analysts covering the stock with top recommendations (it's now "discovered")
- Featured in 3+ national magazines
- Top executives quitting to join rival companies
- PEG exceeds 2
- Same-store sales dropped by more than 3% in the last quarter
- Room for expansion is diminishing

**Key Principle:** "The typical big winner in the Lynch portfolio generally takes three to ten years to play out." Patience is essential — selling a fast grower after a 50% gain means potentially missing a ten-bagger.

> "Moderately fast growers (20 to 25 percent) in nongrowth industries are ideal investments."
> — *One Up on Wall Street*, Chapter 8

### 2.4 Cyclicals

**Definition:** Companies whose sales and profits rise and fall in regular (though not always predictable) patterns. Autos, airlines, steel, chemicals, defense, travel. Timing is everything.

**Characteristics:**
- Revenues and earnings follow economic or industry cycles
- Typically 3-4 bad years followed by 3-4 good years
- P/E ratio is DECEPTIVE — a low P/E on a cyclical often means peak earnings, not a bargain

**Evaluation Criteria:**
- Monitor inventory levels and supply-demand dynamics
- Watch for new market entrants (typically negative)
- Anticipate P/E multiple compression as cycles mature
- Understand industry-specific cycle patterns and timing
- Track early signs of upturn or downturn

**Buy Signal:** Economy is at its weakest, earnings are at their lowest, and public sentiment is at its bleakest. "The worse the slump, the better the recovery."

**Sell Signals:**
- Peak earnings reached — cyclical stocks decline BEFORE reported earnings peak
- Inventories are rising twice as fast as the sales growth rate
- Recession expectations rising
- Stock declines despite strong reported earnings (the market is looking ahead)

**Critical Warning:**
> "You can't go to sleep holding cyclical stocks for a decade and expect to be richly rewarded. The rich rewards are in growth stocks and special situations."
> — Lynch, various articles

**Two-Minute Drill Example (Auto Industry):** "There has been a three-year business slump in the auto industry, but this year things have turned around. I know that because car sales are up..."

### 2.5 Turnarounds

**Definition:** Companies that are doing horribly — potentially going bankrupt — but could bounce back with competent management, innovation, or luck. "No-growers" or "battered" companies.

**Characteristics:**
- Stock price has been severely depressed
- Company may be on the verge of bankruptcy
- High risk but potentially explosive returns — turnarounds are uncorrelated with the general market

**Evaluation Criteria:**
- Can the company survive creditor raids? Examine debt structure
- How much cash does it have? What is the bankruptcy timeline?
- What is shareholder equity value post-restructuring?
- Identify the turnaround mechanism: new management, cost-cutting, divestiture of unprofitable divisions
- Look for business recovery indicators
- Assess impact of cost reduction on profitability

**Buy Signal:** "You know the market is wrong in estimating the magnitude of the crisis." The problems are perceived as worse than they actually are.

**Sell Signal:** When the turnaround story has played out and the stock has re-rated.

**Avoid:** Companies where restructuring announcements mask continued deterioration; companies with heavy bank debt (callable on demand).

**Lynch on Turnarounds:** He sought companies where he understood that the crisis was temporary and the underlying business was sound. The key question: "Are the problems as severe as perceived?"

### 2.6 Asset Plays

**Definition:** Companies sitting on something valuable that the financial community has overlooked or undervalued — real estate, cash, equipment, subscriber bases, tax-loss carryforwards, even brand value.

**Characteristics:**
- The company owns assets worth more than the market realizes
- Often discovered through local knowledge or industry expertise
- Can overlap with other categories

**Evaluation Criteria:**
- Asset valuation: What are the hidden assets worth? (Real estate, cash, patents, subscriber bases)
- Debt burden relative to asset value
- New debt acquisition trends (is management loading up on debt?)
- Potential activist/raider involvement that could unlock shareholder value
- Management's track record of value creation vs. destruction

**Buy Signal:** When you can identify hidden assets that the market has not priced in, and the debt load is manageable.

**Sell Signal:** When the hidden value is recognized and priced into the stock, or when an activist forces value realization.

**Lynch on Asset Plays:** Success requires patience and deep knowledge of the specific assets. Local knowledge gives individual investors a major edge.

## 3. The PEG Ratio — Full Formulation

The PEG ratio is Lynch's signature quantitative metric, described primarily in **Chapter 10 ("Earnings, Earnings, Earnings")** and **Chapter 13 ("Some Famous Numbers")** of *One Up on Wall Street*.

### Formula

```
PEG = P/E Ratio / Annual EPS Growth Rate
```

Where:
- **P/E Ratio** = Current stock price / Earnings per share (trailing or forward)
- **Annual EPS Growth Rate** = Expected annual earnings growth rate (expressed as a whole number, e.g., 25 for 25%)

### Lynch's Rule of Thumb

> "The P/E ratio of any company that's fairly priced will equal its growth rate."
> — *One Up on Wall Street*, Chapter 10

| PEG Value | Lynch's Interpretation |
|-----------|----------------------|
| < 0.5 | Very attractive — significantly undervalued relative to growth |
| < 1.0 | Attractive — growth is not fully priced in |
| = 1.0 | Fairly valued — price reflects growth expectations |
| 1.0-2.0 | Getting expensive — proceed with caution |
| > 2.0 | Overpriced — avoid or sell |

### Refinements

- **P/E at half the growth rate** = "very positive" signal
- **P/E at twice the growth rate** = "very negative" signal
- Lynch also factored in the dividend yield for a more complete picture: **(Growth Rate + Dividend Yield) / P/E > 1** is favorable
- Growth rates above 25% should be treated with skepticism — sustainability is questionable
- The PEG ratio works best for fast growers and stalwarts; it is MISLEADING for cyclicals (where low P/E means peak earnings)

### Lynch's Fair Value Formula

Lynch's complete fair value calculation, as described in *One Up on Wall Street*:

```
Lynch Fair Value = EPS × (Growth Rate + Dividend Yield)
```

If the current price is below this value, the stock is potentially undervalued.

### Example Application

A company earning $2/share, growing at 20% annually, with a 2% dividend yield, and trading at a P/E of 15:
- PEG = 15 / 20 = 0.75 (attractive)
- Lynch Fair Value = $2 × (20 + 2) = $44
- If the stock trades at $30 (P/E of 15), it is below Lynch Fair Value

## 4. Step-by-Step Decision Process

Lynch's analytical sequence can be reconstructed from *One Up on Wall Street* (Chapters 6-15) and his 1994 National Press Club speech.

### Step 1: Notice a Product/Company in Everyday Life

The "invest in what you know" trigger. Lynch found his best ideas not from research reports but from shopping malls, restaurants, and conversations with family.

> "I always thought if you looked at ten companies, you'd find one that's interesting. If you look at a hundred you'll find ten. The person that turns over the most rocks wins."
> — PBS Frontline interview, "Betting on the Market"

**Specific Examples:**
- **L'Eggs (Hanes):** Lynch's wife bought L'Eggs pantyhose at the supermarket. He noticed the product was popular, researched Hanes, and it became Magellan's largest position. When worried about competition from "No Nonsense" brand, he bought 62 pairs of competing pantyhose, distributed them to colleagues, and they reported the quality was inferior. (*One Up on Wall Street*; Wharton Magazine interview)
- **Dunkin' Donuts:** Lynch stopped for coffee, noticed the stores were always crowded, discovered thousands of new franchises were opening, and invested before 40 consecutive quarters of positive earnings growth. Made 10-15x his money. (*1994 National Press Club speech*)
- **Taco Bell:** Fell from $14 to $1 in the early 1970s; Lynch bought at $7, it became Magellan's largest position in 1978 when Pepsi acquired it at $42. Lynch believed it would have gone to $400 without the acquisition. (*1994 speech*)

### Step 2: Classify Into One of Six Categories

> "Figure out what kind of stock it is, closely follow and then make decisions. Putting into categories helps in defining the first story."
> — *One Up on Wall Street*, Chapter 7

This classification determines EVERYTHING about how you evaluate the stock: what metrics matter, what the buy/sell signals are, and what returns you should expect. (See Section 2 for complete taxonomy.)

### Step 3: Check the "Story" — Is It Simple and Understandable?

> "Never invest in any idea you can't illustrate with a crayon."
> — *One Up on Wall Street*

> "If you can't explain to a 10-year-old in two minutes or less why you own a stock, you shouldn't own it."
> — 1994 National Press Club speech

The story must be a simple narrative: "Dunkin' Donuts is opening thousands of new locations and has 40 straight quarters of earnings growth." Not: "The macroeconomic tailwinds in the QSR sector will drive enterprise value accretion."

### Step 4: Calculate the PEG Ratio

Apply the PEG framework (Section 3) appropriate to the stock's category. For fast growers and stalwarts, PEG < 1 is the threshold. For cyclicals, do NOT use PEG — use industry cycle position instead.

### Step 5: Check the Balance Sheet

> "It's debt that determines which companies will survive and which will go bankrupt in a crisis. Young companies with heavy debts are always at risk."
> — *One Up on Wall Street*, Chapter 13

Key balance sheet checks:
- **Cash position:** Strong cash reserves provide downside protection. A $20 stock with $10/share in cash has significant hidden value.
- **Debt-to-equity ratio:** Normal balance is 75% equity, 25% debt.
- **Type of debt matters:** Bank debt (worst — callable on demand) vs. Funded debt (best — long-term, cannot be called).
- **Free cash flow:** Preferred metric over earnings alone for assessing quality.
- **Inventory trends:** Inventory growth exceeding sales growth is a red flag.

### Step 6: Evaluate Earnings Trajectory and Institutional Ownership

- **Earnings record:** Assess consistency and growth trajectory. 20 consecutive quarters of growth is a strong signal.
- **Institutional ownership:** Lower is better — means the stock is undiscovered.
- **Analyst coverage:** Fewer analysts = more opportunity for the individual investor.
- **Insider buying:** Management buying their own stock with personal money is a very positive signal.

> "Look for companies with little or no institutional ownership."
> — *One Up on Wall Street*, Chapter 8

### Step 7: Apply the Two-Minute Drill

*One Up on Wall Street*, Chapter 11: "The Two-Minute Drill"

Before buying, deliver a concise monologue covering:
1. The reasons you are interested in the company
2. What must happen for the company to succeed
3. The obstacles standing in its path

This monologue must be tailored to the stock's category. A two-minute drill for a cyclical will focus on industry cycle timing; for a fast grower, it will focus on expansion runway and earnings trajectory.

> "The more one knows, the better."
> — *One Up on Wall Street*, Chapter 11

### 5.3 Communication Style Characteristics

**Conversational and anecdotal:** Lynch writes and speaks as if telling stories to a friend. He peppers his analysis with personal anecdotes (caddying at the golf course, shopping with his wife, stopping at Dunkin' Donuts).

**Humor and self-deprecation:** "Apple was not that hard to understand. I mean, how dumb was I?" (CNBC, 2023). He freely admits his failures and long shots that went to zero.

**Consumer analogies over financial jargon:** Instead of discussing "enterprise value multiples," Lynch talks about how many donuts Dunkin' sells and how many new stores are opening.

**Baseball metaphors:** The term "ten-bagger" itself is a baseball metaphor (bases). He uses batting average analogies: being right 6 out of 10 times is Hall of Fame caliber.

**Garden metaphors:** "Cutting the flowers and watering the weeds" for the mistake of selling winners and keeping losers.

**Speaking to individuals, NOT institutions:** Lynch's audience is always the amateur investor, the person at the cocktail party, the consumer in the mall. He deliberately demystifies investing and positions it as accessible to anyone with common sense and patience.

**Enthusiasm and energy:** Lynch's public appearances are characterized by rapid-fire delivery, genuine excitement about specific companies, and an infectious optimism about the long-term trajectory of stocks.

## 6. Contrastive Beliefs: What Lynch Rejects and How He Differs

### 6.1 What Lynch Explicitly Rejects

**Macroeconomic forecasting as a basis for stock picking:**
> "If you spend more than 13 minutes analyzing economic and market forecasts, you've wasted 10 minutes."

> "You never can predict the economy. You can't predict the stock market."
> — Wall Street Week

In his 1994 speech, Lynch noted that nobody predicted the 1982 recession with 14% inflation, 12% unemployment, and a 20% prime rate.

**The Efficient Market Hypothesis:**
> "Efficient markets? That's a bunch of junk, crazy stuff." — Fortune, 1995

At Wharton, Lynch came to believe that the EMH was contradictory. His 29.2% annual return over 13 years is itself a refutation.

**The idea that individuals can't beat the market:**
Lynch's entire body of work is built on the premise that amateur investors have a structural edge — local knowledge, consumer experience, lack of institutional constraints. He believed smaller individual investors had advantages because institutions couldn't invest in smaller companies.

**Over-diversification (for individual investors):**
> "The part-time stock picker probably has time to follow 8-12 companies."

> "A foolish diversity is the hobgoblin of small investors."
> — *One Up on Wall Street*, Chapter 16

> "Owning stocks is like having children — don't get involved with more than you can handle."

(Note: Lynch himself held 1,000+ stocks at Magellan, but he was a full-time professional with a research army. He advised individuals to own only what they could truly understand.)

**Market timing:**
> "A market player has 50 percent of his portfolio in cash at the bottom of the market. When the market moves up, he can miss most of the move."

> "People who exit the stock market to avoid a decline are odds-on favorites to miss the next rally."

> "If timing the market is such a great strategy, why haven't we seen the names of any market timers at the top of the Forbes list of richest Americans?"

**Stop-losses:**
Lynch opposed them. Stop-losses take you out at the worst time. His philosophy was that if you understand the company and the story is intact, a price drop is a buying opportunity, not a selling signal.

**Hot stocks in hot industries:**
> "Avoid hot stocks in hot industries." — *One Up on Wall Street*, Chapter 9

> "Be suspicious of companies with growth rates of 50 to 100 percent a year."

**"Next [Company Name]" stocks:**
Lynch avoided companies marketed as "the next IBM" or "the next McDonald's." They almost never are.

**Whisper stocks:**
Secretive tips on speculative companies. "I broke even on zero of thirty long shots I purchased." — 1994 speech

### 6.2 Lynch vs. Warren Buffett

| Dimension | Peter Lynch | Warren Buffett |
|-----------|------------|----------------|
| **Core approach** | Growth at a Reasonable Price (GARP) | Quality at a fair price (buy wonderful companies) |
| **Stock taxonomy** | Six categories with different frameworks for each | One archetype: companies with durable competitive advantages ("moats") |
| **Active hunting** | Actively hunts for ten-baggers; turns over thousands of rocks | Waits patiently for the "fat pitch" — quality companies at rare discounts |
| **Holding period** | 1-5 years for stalwarts; 3-10 years for fast growers | "Our favorite holding period is forever" |
| **Portfolio size** | Held 1,000+ stocks at Magellan; advises individuals to hold 8-12 | Concentrated portfolio of 15-25 positions |
| **Growth tolerance** | Actively seeks 20-25% growers; willing to pay up for growth | Prefers predictable, steady compounders; suspicious of high growth |
| **Primary metric** | PEG ratio | Intrinsic value (discounted future cash flows) |
| **Edge** | Consumer observation + volume research | Deep understanding of competitive moats + patience |
| **Cyclicals** | Actively trades cyclicals based on timing | Generally avoids cyclicals as unpredictable |
| **Communication** | Enthusiastic, anecdotal, consumer-focused | Folksy but more analytical; uses insurance/probability language |

### 6.3 Lynch vs. Benjamin Graham

| Dimension | Peter Lynch | Benjamin Graham |
|-----------|------------|-----------------|
| **Core approach** | GARP — growth with valuation discipline | Deep value — buy assets below liquidation value |
| **Qualitative vs. quantitative** | Embraces qualitative judgment (consumer experience, story, management quality) | Relies on quantitative formulas and statistical screening |
| **Growth stance** | Actively seeks and pays for growth | Suspicious of growth projections; focuses on current tangible assets |
| **Valuation tool** | PEG ratio (forward-looking) | Net Current Asset Value, P/B, P/E screens (backward-looking) |
| **Portfolio approach** | Concentrated in understood companies | Statistical baskets of cheap stocks (cigar butts) |
| **Individual stocks** | Deep research on each holding; "know the story" | Basket approach; the portfolio is the edge, not individual conviction |
| **Time horizon** | 1-10 years depending on category | Hold until market recognizes value (typically 2-3 years) |

### 6.4 Lynch vs. Charlie Munger

| Dimension | Peter Lynch | Charlie Munger |
|-----------|------------|----------------|
| **Core approach** | GARP with six-category taxonomy | Quality compounders — "buy wonderful businesses at fair prices" |
| **Mental models** | Category-specific evaluation frameworks | Multidisciplinary latticework of mental models (psychology, physics, biology) |
| **Holding philosophy** | Rotate stalwarts after 30-50% gain; hold fast growers longer | "The big money is in the waiting" — hold great businesses almost indefinitely |
| **Portfolio** | Massive diversification (professionally); 8-12 stocks (for amateurs) | Extreme concentration — "a well-diversified portfolio needs only 3 stocks" |
| **Circle of competence** | Expand it aggressively — turn over rocks, explore new industries | Stay strictly within it — avoid anything you don't deeply understand |
| **Key metric** | PEG ratio | Return on invested capital (ROIC) + competitive position |

### 6.5 Lynch vs. Howard Marks

| Dimension | Peter Lynch | Howard Marks |
|-----------|------------|--------------|
| **Core approach** | GARP — find growth at reasonable prices | Value with deep risk consciousness — buy assets for less than they're worth |
| **Primary concern** | Finding the next ten-bagger | Controlling risk — "the real skill is to make money with risk under control" |
| **Market view** | Markets are inefficient; individual investors have an edge | Markets are inefficient at extremes; second-level thinking required |
| **Risk definition** | Risk is not knowing what you own; risk is selling a ten-bagger too early | Risk is the probability of permanent loss of capital, not volatility |
| **Cycles** | Uses cyclicals as one of six categories; trades cycles | Cycles are central to the entire philosophy; market pendulums swing between greed and fear |
| **Communication** | Enthusiastic, anecdotal, optimistic | Measured, philosophical, cautious |
| **Emotional stance** | Optimistic — "profits go up 8% a year, stocks will follow" | Cautious — "the most dangerous thing is to think there's no risk" |

### 6.6 Lynch vs. Li Lu

| Dimension | Peter Lynch | Li Lu |
|-----------|------------|-------|
| **Core approach** | GARP in primarily US equities | Value investing applied to emerging markets, especially China |
| **Geographic focus** | Domestic US market; Main Street consumer observation | Global, with particular focus on underfished ponds (China, Asia) |
| **Opportunity source** | Consumer observation + volume research | Deep fundamental analysis in markets with less institutional competition |
| **Holding period** | Category-dependent (1-10 years) | Very long-term; concentrated positions held for decades |
| **Portfolio** | Extremely diversified (professionally) | Highly concentrated — a few high-conviction positions |
| **Key influence** | Self-developed GARP framework | Graham's value principles + Munger's quality framework |
| **Edge** | Amateur's consumer knowledge edge | Information/access edge in less efficient emerging markets |

## 7. Risk Framework

Lynch's risk framework is distinctive because it defines risk NOT as volatility or beta, but as **ignorance** — not knowing what you own.

### 7.1 Core Risk Principles

**Know what you own and why you own it:**
> "Know what you own, and know why you own it." — *One Up on Wall Street*

> "The worst thing you can do is invest in companies you know nothing about." — *One Up on Wall Street*

If you can't pass the Two-Minute Drill, you don't understand the investment well enough, and that IS the risk.

**The biggest risk is missing a ten-bagger by selling too early:**
> "Some stocks go up 20-30 percent and they get rid of it and hold onto the dogs. It's like watering the weeds and cutting out the flowers."
> — PBS Frontline

Lynch's framework inverts conventional risk thinking: the danger isn't losing money on a bad stock (you can only lose 100%), but missing the 10x, 20x, or 40x return on a great stock by selling prematurely.

> "All you need for a lifetime of successful investing is a few big winners."

> "Maybe you're right 5 or 6 times out of 10. But if your winners go up 4- or 10- or 20-fold, it makes up for the ones where you lost 50%, 75%, or 100%."
> — *Lessons from an Investing Legend* interview

### 7.2 Specific Risk Controls

**Diversify across categories, not just companies:**
Don't load up on fast growers alone. Keep stalwarts for recession protection, some cyclicals for economic recovery exposure, and perhaps a turnaround for asymmetric upside. Lynch's suggested portfolio allocation (for small portfolios):
- 30-40% in growth stocks
- 10-20% in stalwarts
- 10-20% in cyclicals
- Remainder in turnarounds

**Balance sheet as risk filter:**
> "Seek out depressed stocks in troubled companies with superior financial positions, and avoid ones with loads of bank debt."
> — *One Up on Wall Street*, Chapter 13

- Normal debt/equity: 75% equity, 25% debt
- Bank debt is the most dangerous (callable on demand)
- Funded/bonded debt is safer (long-term, cannot be called)
- Cash position is downside protection

**Never use stop-losses:**
Stop-losses take you out at the bottom. If the story is intact and the company is sound, a price decline is an opportunity, not a reason to sell.

**Expect to be wrong often:**
> "In this business, if you're good, you're right six times out of ten." — PBS Frontline

> "There's no shame in losing money on a stock. Everybody does it. The shameful thing is to hold on when fundamentals are deteriorating." — *One Up on Wall Street*

**Market declines are opportunities, not disasters:**
> "A correction is a wonderful opportunity to buy your favorite companies at a bargain price."

> "When I'm down 25 percent, I'm a buyer." — Various interviews

> "Far more money has been lost by investors preparing for corrections, or trying to anticipate corrections, than has been lost in corrections themselves." — *Learn to Earn*

### 7.3 What NOT to Buy (Lynch's Red Flags)

- Hottest stocks in hottest industries
- "The next [IBM/McDonald's/Intel]" candidates
- Whisper stocks with emotional appeal but no substance
- Companies with exciting names but mediocre fundamentals
- Penny stocks ("equal loss potential as expensive stocks")
- Companies where you can't explain the business to a child
- Companies with growth rates above 50% (unsustainable)
- Stocks everyone loves — 40 analysts with buy ratings

### 7.4 Twelve Dangerous Myths Lynch Debunks

From *One Up on Wall Street*, Chapter 17 area:
1. "If it's gone down this much already, it can't go much lower"
2. "You can always tell when a stock's hit the bottom"
3. "If it's gone this high already, how can it possibly go higher?"
4. "It's only $3 a share — what can I lose?"
5. "Eventually they always come back"
6. "It's always darkest before the dawn"
7. "When it rebounds to $10, I'll sell"
8. "What me worry? Conservative stocks don't fluctuate much"
9. "It's taking too long for anything to ever happen"
10. "Look at all the money I've lost: I didn't buy it!"
11. "I missed that one, I'll catch the next one"
12. "The stock's gone up, so I must be right" / "The stock's gone down, so I must be wrong"

## 9. The 13 Attributes of a Perfect Stock

From *One Up on Wall Street*, Chapter 8: "The Perfect Stock, What a Deal!"

1. **It has a dull or ridiculous name** — keeps casual investors and institutions away
2. **It does something dull** — boring operations reduce competition for the stock
3. **It does something disagreeable** — e.g., waste management, funeral homes — repels people
4. **It's a spinoff** — recently separated companies often outperform; management has something to prove
5. **Institutions don't own it and analysts don't follow it** — undiscovered = opportunity
6. **There are negative rumors** — toxic industries, controversial associations create unwarranted discounts
7. **There's something depressing about it** — mortality-related or unappealing sectors
8. **It's in a no-growth industry** — limited competition from new entrepreneurs
9. **It's got a niche** — exclusive franchise or competitive moat (toll bridge businesses)
10. **People have to keep buying it** — consumables requiring repeat purchases (razors, drugs, cigarettes)
11. **It's a user of technology** — benefits from tech cost reductions without the risk of being a tech company
12. **Insiders are buying** — company executives investing personal funds is a strong positive signal
13. **The company is buying back shares** — reduces share count, increases EPS

> "All else being equal, invest in the company with the fewest color photographs in the annual report."
> — *One Up on Wall Street*, Chapter 8

## 10. Lynch's 25 Golden Rules

From Peter Lynch's published rules (various sources):

1. Investing is fun, exciting, and dangerous if you don't do any work.
2. Your investor's edge is not something you get from Wall Street experts. It's something you already have.
3. Behind every stock is a company. Find out what it's doing.
4. Often, there is no correlation between the success of a company's operations and the success of its stock over a few months or even a few years. In the long term, there is a 100 percent correlation.
5. You have to know what you own, and why you own it. "This baby is a cinch to go up!" doesn't count.
6. Long shots almost always miss the mark.
7. Owning stocks is like having children — don't get involved with more than you can handle.
8. If you can't find any companies that you think are attractive, put your money in the bank until you discover some.
9. Never invest in a company without understanding its finances.
10. Avoid hot stocks in hot industries.
11. With small companies, you're better off to wait until they turn a profit before you invest.
12. If you're thinking about investing in a troubled industry, buy the companies with staying power. Also, wait for the industry to show signs of revival.
13. If you invest $1,000 in a stock, all you can lose is $1,000, but you stand to gain $10,000 or even $50,000 over time if you're patient.
14. In every industry and every region of the country, the observant amateur can find great growth companies long before the professionals have discovered them.
15. A stock-market decline is as routine as a January blizzard in Colorado. If you're prepared, it can't hurt you. A decline is a great opportunity to pick up the bargains left behind by investors who are fleeing the storm in panic.
16. Everyone has the brainpower to make money in stocks. Not everyone has the stomach.
17. Nobody can predict interest rates, the future direction of the economy, or the stock market. Dismiss all such forecasts and concentrate on what's actually happening to the companies in which you've invested.
18. If you study 10 companies, you'll find 1 for which the story is better than expected. If you study 50, you'll find 5. There are always pleasant surprises to be found in the stock market.
19. If you don't study any companies, you have the same success buying stocks as you do in a poker game if you bet without looking at your cards.
20. Time is on your side when you own shares of superior companies.
21. If you don't find "ten-bagger" stocks in your own portfolio, you'll find them in other portfolios. Be patient.
22. Big companies have small moves, small companies have big moves.
23. You won't improve results by pulling out the flowers and watering the weeds.
24. If the company story is still good, keep the stock. If the story has changed, sell.
25. When favorable cards turn up, add to your bet, and vice versa.

## 11. The Cocktail Party Theory (Market Cycle Indicator)

From *One Up on Wall Street*, Chapter 5: "Is This a Good Market? Please Don't Ask"

Lynch developed this theory "over years of standing in the middle of living rooms, near punch bowls, listening to what the nearest ten people said about stocks."

### Four Stages

**Stage 1 — Market Bottom (The Dentist Phase):**
The market has been down for a while. Nobody talks about stocks. When Lynch mentions he runs an equity mutual fund, the guest nods politely and walks away. "Folks are more interested in talking to a dentist about plaque than to Lynch about stocks." **This is the best time to buy.**

**Stage 2 — Early Recovery:**
The market is up modestly. Guests linger a moment when Lynch says he runs an equity fund, but only to comment on "how risky the market is." They still prefer talking to the dentist. **Still a good time to buy.**

**Stage 3 — Bull Market (Tips Begin):**
The market has been up substantially. A crowd gathers around Lynch at the party. People are no longer interested in the dentist — they want stock tips from Lynch. **Time to be cautious.**

**Stage 4 — Market Top (Everyone's an Expert):**
People are crowded around Lynch, but now THEY are telling HIM what stocks to buy. The dentist is offering stock tips. Neighbors and taxi drivers recommend stocks. **This is a strong sell signal — the market is near a top.**

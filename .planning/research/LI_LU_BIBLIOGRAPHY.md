## 1. Philosophy Distillation: What Makes Li Lu UNIQUELY Li Lu

Li Lu is not simply "Buffett applied to China," though that label follows him everywhere. He is a genuine philosophical synthesizer who has built an investment framework that bridges Western value investing theory with a deep, historically-grounded understanding of Chinese and emerging market dynamics. What makes Li Lu unique is the combination of: (a) a lived experience of civilizational upheaval that gives his macro perspective existential weight, (b) a scholarly framework connecting modernization theory to investment, and (c) a cross-cultural analytical edge that no purely Western or purely Chinese investor can replicate.

Li Lu was born in Tangshan, China in 1966. He survived the 1976 Tangshan earthquake -- one of the deadliest in recorded history. During the Cultural Revolution, his parents were sent to labor camps, and he was passed through multiple orphanages. In 1989, he became a student leader in the Tiananmen Square protests, fled China after the crackdown, arrived in New York at age 23, and simultaneously earned a B.A. in economics, a J.D., and an M.B.A. from Columbia University. He was "inspired to get into investment after hearing Warren Buffett give a lecture at Columbia in 1993," finding Buffett's principles "concise, logical and convincing."

This biography matters because it is not incidental to his philosophy -- it IS his philosophy. Li Lu's approach to investing is fundamentally about understanding civilizational transformation, because he has lived through one. His 2015 Peking University lecture, his 2020 book "Civilization, Modernization, Value Investing and China," and his 2024 speech "Global Value Investing in Our Era" all present a unified framework that begins not with Graham's balance sheet analysis but with human civilizational development.

**The Civilizations Framework**: Li Lu divides human history into three eras: Civilization 1.0 (hunter-gatherers), Civilization 2.0 (agricultural societies), and Civilization 3.0 (modern science-technology era, post-1750s). The pivotal year was 1776, when Adam Smith's "Wealth of Nations" established free market economics, the American Declaration of Independence established rule of law, and the Watt steam engine commercialized the industrial revolution. The combination of the Scientific Revolution and the market economy produced "continuous, compound economic growth never before seen in human history."

This is not mere historical musing -- it is the foundation of Li Lu's investment thesis. He defines modernization as "the phenomenon that the whole economy begins to enter a state of continuous progress and unlimited growth." And he argues: "The process of modernization itself is actually compound interest, and the essence of value investing is the same as modernization." This is a radically original contribution to value investing theory. Where Graham gave us margin of safety, Buffett gave us moats, Munger gave us mental models, and Marks gave us cycles, Li Lu gives us a civilizational theory of why compound growth exists at all -- and where to find it.

Li Lu places China at "Civilization 2.5" -- a transitional phase between agricultural and modern -- which means China's modernization is incomplete but irreversible. This creates the core investment opportunity: "China remains one of the best markets if you are a value investor. The market is still underdeveloped and as a result not representative of the real economy compared to the US." The inefficiency is structural, not transient. Chinese markets are characterized by "a mentality of fast trading and high turnover," weak disclosure standards, government-approved IPO processes that distort listings, and a retail-dominated investor base. For a patient, knowledgeable value investor, this is fertile ground.

**The Evolution from Graham to Munger, Applied to Asia**: Li Lu began as a pure Graham deep-value investor, buying stocks below net asset value. He describes seeking "obvious value primarily on the balance sheet in the classic style of Benjamin Graham." But through his partnership with Munger (who invested $88 million in Himalaya Capital in 2004 on the condition the fund remain closed to new investors), he evolved toward identifying "great companies in Asia with an enduring competitive advantage and a long growth trajectory ahead of them." This mirrors Buffett's own evolution, but Li Lu applied it to markets where the transition from cigar-butt investing to quality investing is still underway -- Asian markets still offer both types of opportunities.

**The Core Insight -- "Take the Macro as It Is"**: Li Lu's most distinctive operational principle is his approach to macroeconomics. He states: "The macro environment is an objective reality; we can only accept it. The micro level is where we can actually do something." And: "Our investment is about taking the world as it is...and then acting on specific companies where we have real insight." He classifies macroeconomic assessment as "too hard" -- preferring to acknowledge macro tailwinds and headwinds without obsessing over magnitude or timing. This is more radical than Buffett's famous macro agnosticism, because Li Lu operates in markets (China, emerging Asia) where macro forces are far more volatile and politically-driven. His point is not that macro doesn't matter -- it's that you cannot profitably predict it, so you must build a portfolio that thrives regardless.

**The Fund Structure as Philosophy**: Upon Munger's advice, Li Lu restructured Himalaya Capital from a conventional hedge fund into a long-only investment vehicle modeled on early Buffett partnerships. The fund charges no annual fixed management fee on index-like returns. Most of Li Lu's personal wealth is invested in the fund. Investors commit to long-term horizons, and the fund is not open to new investors. Since inception in 1998, Himalaya has compounded at approximately 30% annually -- not through constant activity, but through highly selective, concentrated action.

## 2. Mental Models Catalog

### 2.1 "Take the Macro as It Is" (Macro Acceptance, Micro Agency)

**Definition**: Accept macroeconomic conditions as objective reality. Direct all analytical energy toward micro-level company analysis where genuine insight is possible.

**Li Lu's formulation**: "The macro environment is an objective reality; we can only accept it. The micro level is where we can actually do something. This is the basic stance of a value investor."

**Extended articulation**: "Value investing...is inevitably tied to its era...we cannot escape the time in which we live." However: "The world is objective and will not change because of our wishes or subjective judgments." Investors should not "overly research the macro environment or thoroughly research entire economies." Instead: "They need to find which lakes have fish, where there is a lack of competition, where they have good knowledge and an advantage."

**Operational implication**: Map macro trends as context. Assess their impact on specific companies. But never let macro narratives become the primary investment driver. This is particularly important in China, where macro headlines (trade wars, regulatory crackdowns, demographic shifts) dominate Western coverage and create persistent mispricings for patient analysts who focus on business fundamentals.

**How this differs from others**: Buffett simply ignores macro ("I never have an opinion about the market"). Marks explicitly tracks the macro cycle as his primary signal. Li Lu acknowledges macro reality but treats it as immovable backdrop -- neither ignoring it nor trying to profit from it.

**Source**: "Global Value Investing in Our Era" (December 2024, Peking University); Fireside chat with Bruce Greenwald at Columbia Business School (2021).

### 2.2 The Civilizations Framework (1.0, 2.0, 3.0)

**Definition**: Human economic history progresses through three civilizational stages. The transition to Civilization 3.0 (science + free markets) produces unprecedented, sustained compound growth. Value investing is the optimal strategy for participating in this compounding.

**Three stages**:
- **Civilization 1.0**: Hunter-gatherer societies. Zero-sum resource competition.
- **Civilization 2.0**: Agricultural societies. Growth constrained by land and natural forces. System characterized by shortages.
- **Civilization 3.0**: Modern science-technology era. Free markets + scientific method = continuous compound growth. "Unlimited growth" becomes possible for the first time.

**The key mechanism**: Free trade produces "1+1>2" outcomes (both parties benefit from exchange). Free exchange of knowledge and ideas produces "1+1>4" outcomes: "When two parties exchange ideas, they obtain the ideas of the other while retaining their own, which may spark entirely new ideas." This compounding of knowledge is what drives sustained economic growth.

**Investment application**: "Modernization represents a shift in the civilization paradigm. This shift is unstoppable and cannot be reversed." Countries entering Civilization 3.0 (China at "2.5," plus much of Southeast Asia, South America) represent enormous opportunities because they are entering the compound-growth phase. "We hope to make efforts toward the global application of value investment. After all, eighty to ninety percent of people live outside developed countries."

**Source**: "The Prospect of Value Investing in China" (October 2015, Peking University); "Civilization, Modernization, Value Investing and China" (book, 2020); "Global Value Investing in Our Era" (December 2024).

### 2.3 China/Emerging Markets as Structural Inefficiency Frontier

**Definition**: Chinese and emerging markets contain persistent structural mispricings due to market immaturity, not transient conditions. These inefficiencies create a durable edge for disciplined value investors.

**Li Lu's formulation**: "China remains one of the best markets if you are a value investor. The market is still underdeveloped and as a result not representative of the real economy compared to the US." This "usually provides opportunities for those mature patient investors who truly know what they're doing."

**Sources of inefficiency**:
- Retail-dominated investor base with "mentality of fast trading and high turnover"
- Government-approved IPO processes creating distorted listing composition
- Weak disclosure standards and variable accounting trustworthiness
- Indices that "don't fairly represent the economy due to weak disclosure and poor de-listing"
- Western investors systematically misjudging Chinese government-market dynamics based on headlines
- Emerging consumer economy transitioning from export-driven to domestic-driven

**The cross-cultural edge**: Li Lu possesses "competitive advantages that purely Western or purely Chinese investors lack, creating cultural arbitrage that is invisible to other investors." He serves as "a translator between China's institutional realities and global investor expectations, rooted in both insider knowledge of China's institutional landscape and rigorous Western financial training."

**Source**: Columbia Business School fireside chat (2021); Graham & Doddsville interview (Spring 2013); "The Prospect of Value Investing in China" (2015).

### 2.4 Intellectual Honesty as Survival Mechanism

**Definition**: The most important quality in an investor is radical honesty about the boundaries of one's knowledge. The market systematically destroys those who pretend to know more than they do.

**Li Lu's canonical formulation**: "Investing is about intellectual honesty. You want to know what you know. You want to know, mostly, what you don't know."

**The four categories of knowledge** (from the 2013 Graham & Doddsville interview): Li Lu stresses four critical distinctions: knowing what you know, acknowledging gaps in knowledge, understanding what doesn't require knowledge, and recognizing unknown unknowns. "Crises reveal which investors maintained these distinctions beforehand."

**The market as ruthless examiner**: "The market is a mechanism that discovers your weaknesses. Any fault/defect you have will be magnified infinitely, to the point of complete destruction." And: "If you just pretend you know a business well, you will be tested in difficult times."

**The rationalization trap**: "Now we're very good at rationalization, but we're really not very good at being rational." Human evolutionary psychology makes true objectivity extraordinarily difficult, which is why intellectual honesty must be treated as a moral discipline, not merely a cognitive exercise.

**The compounding benefit**: "The more honest you are intellectually, the more prosperous you tend to be." And: "Knowledge accumulates gradually but only if you maintain intellectual honesty...your knowledge accumulating in the same way the economy grows through a process of compounding where past experiences corroborate and reinforce each other."

**Source**: Columbia Business School lecture (2006); San Francisco State FAME talk (2012); Graham & Doddsville interview (2013); "The Practice of Value Investing" (Peking University, 2019).

### 2.5 The Owner's Mentality

**Definition**: View every stock as if you inherited 100% of the business. This transforms how you research, evaluate, and hold investments.

**Li Lu's formulation**: "You don't think of yourself as a paper shuffler...You think of yourself as a real owner of the business." He instructs his analysts: "Imagine...you have inherited 100% of a business...you would really seek out knowledge on how it should be run."

**Practical application**: "Once you start seeing yourself as an owner, you'll have the energy needed to study the business." Most investors treat stocks as "pieces of paper to be traded" because trading is easy. But if you inherited the entire business, "you would not be trading -- you would seek out knowledge on how it should be run and how it works, which helps you determine the business's worth."

**The test of understanding**: "Articulate the worst-case scenario for a company over ten years. If you cannot, you can't really say you understand the company." He references Munger's principle: hold opinions only if you can refute them better than the smartest opposing voice.

**Source**: Columbia Business School lectures (2006, 2010); Q&A at Peking University value investing course; "The Practice of Value Investing."

### 2.6 Risk as Permanent Capital Loss (Not Volatility)

**Definition**: The real risk in investing is permanent loss of capital, not temporary price fluctuations. Price drops are opportunities, not dangers.

**Li Lu's canonical formulation**: "In my view, the biggest investment risk is not the volatility of prices, but whether you will suffer a permanent loss of capital. Not only is the mere drop in stock prices not risk, but it is an opportunity. Where else do you look for cheap stocks?"

**Practical application**: Even Berkshire Hathaway has experienced 50%+ price declines multiple times. "The hallmark of a good investor is if you can watch your portfolio go down 50% and not be affected at all." During BYD's 47% plunge in 2014, Li Lu increased his position rather than selling. This requires deep understanding of the business -- you can only hold through drawdowns if you genuinely know what you own.

**The diversification corollary**: Being too diversified means you don't know your holdings well enough. Real safety comes from deep knowledge, not position spreading. Li Lu's top 5 positions constitute ~94% of his portfolio.

**Source**: "The Prospect of Value Investing in China" (2015); Columbia Business School fireside chat (2021); demonstrated through BYD position management.

### 2.7 "Fish Where the Fish Are"

**Definition**: Focus research and capital on markets, sectors, and situations where structural conditions create persistent mispricings and low competition from other skilled investors.

**Li Lu's formulation**: Investors must "fish where the fish are" -- concentrating on areas where genuine value opportunities exist due to structural inefficiencies, not trying to find bargains in efficiently-priced markets.

**Application**: This explains Li Lu's geographic focus. Rather than competing in the hyper-efficient U.S. large-cap market, he concentrates on Chinese and Asian markets where the investor base is less sophisticated, information asymmetries are larger, and the structural conditions create persistent discounts. "China is emerging to become the most dynamic, fastest growing consumer market in the whole world" -- yet its stock markets remain dominated by short-term speculators.

**Knowledge as moat**: "Your circle of competence will inevitably be small. Making money doesn't depend on how much you know; it depends on whether what you know is right or wrong." Focus on being deeply right about a few things rather than superficially right about many.

**Source**: Graham & Doddsville interview (2013); "Global Value Investing in Our Era" (2024); Columbia Business School lecture (2006).

### 2.8 The Fat Pitch / Swing Hard

**Definition**: Wait patiently for rare opportunities with overwhelming odds in your favor, then commit aggressively. Most of the time, do nothing.

**Li Lu's formulation**: "Once in a while, you will find a 'fat pitch' that is slow, straight, and right in the middle of your sweet spot. Then you swing hard. This way, no matter what natural ability you start with, you will substantially increase your hitting average."

**The opposite error is equally dangerous**: "The opposite problem is equally harmful to long-term results: You discover a 'fat pitch' but are unable to swing with the full weight of your capital."

**The patience requirement**: "You don't have to swing a lot [as an investor]. You study all the time, but you don't have to do anything." Opportunities don't arrive on schedule. Years may pass without investment activity. When they appear, intensive research must happen quickly and conviction must drive decisive action. Munger "read Barron's for 50 years but made only one investment from it."

**The probability framework**: "If you always swing when you have an overwhelmingly better edge, then over time, you will do very well." This is probability-based decision making -- seeking overwhelming evidence before committing capital.

**Source**: Columbia Business School masterclass (2006); Graham & Doddsville interview (2013); Q&A with Li Lu at Peking University.

### 2.9 Enduring Competitive Advantage + Long Growth Trajectory

**Definition**: The ideal investment combines a business with sustainable competitive advantages and a long runway of growth ahead. Both conditions must be present.

**Li Lu's formulation**: He looks for "truly exceptional businesses...the ones that can fend off competitors, who have an enduring competitive advantage" and maintain "higher than above average return on capital" with "a long growth trajectory ahead."

**How to evaluate**: "The nature of a business' competition such that one could predict the outcome in 10 years, even with all the ups and downs in the macro environment" -- this is "the most important" criterion. The test: "You probably cannot predict the company's growth more than 10 years into the future...However, you can probably predict with high degree of confidence the worst case scenario for selected companies and industries over the next ten years."

**Sources of competitive advantage**: Scale economies in some industries; brand loyalty and cultural advantages in others; management capability to reallocate capital; high switching costs (Bloomberg terminals as example); network effects and winner-take-all dynamics; vertical integration (BYD's supply chain control).

**On change**: "The only constant is the constant change. All great businesses change over time and no business can remain that competitive edge forever, but some can keep it for a long time."

**Source**: Columbia Business School fireside chat (2021); Columbia lecture (2006); "The Practice of Value Investing."

### 2.10 The Specialist-Generalist Balance

**Definition**: Be a generalist in learning across all disciplines. Become a specialist when investing in specific companies.

**Li Lu's formulation**: "You want to be a specialist in the company you choose to invest, but a generalist of business, so that your circle of competence can constantly evolve and enlarge over time."

**Cross-disciplinary learning**: "You have to learn everything...Politics, science, technology, literature...everything can affect businesses." He credits his wife's PhD in biology for providing investment insights. The key is that opportunities emerge from deep knowledge across disciplines, not just financial analysis.

**Application**: Li Lu eliminates "approximately 99,000 of 100,000 global stocks, focusing only on predictable industries with exceptional companies." The generalist knowledge helps identify which industries to specialize in; the specialist knowledge generates the conviction required for concentrated positions.

**Source**: Columbia Business School lecture (2010); Q&A at Peking University; Columbia masterclass (2006).

### 2.11 Value Investing as "Faith" (A Way of Life)

**Definition**: Value investing is not merely a technique -- it is a belief system about creating value for society rather than extracting it from others.

**Li Lu's formulation**: Value investing represents "a set of beliefs where you aren't willing to exploit others, won't participate in zero-sum games, and will only pursue fortune in a way that also benefits society." Speculative gains "sum to zero across all participants -- it's purely redistributive. Only investing in genuine economic growth creates wealth."

**The information exploitation tax**: Li Lu identifies a pervasive problem in the investment industry where the fee structure exploits uninformed investors -- what he calls the "information exploitation tax." His fund structure (no management fee on index-like returns, aligned incentives) is his rejection of this exploitation.

**The ethical dimension**: He emphasizes treating client capital as "money your parents worked hard to save." Munger's influence is visible here: "He insisted on making money in the most morally sound way, entering transactions only when, if positions were reversed, he would comfortably take the other side."

**Investing as breathing with the times**: Li Lu frames value investing as "a way to breathe in sync with the times and grow together with it" -- balancing rational analysis with patient, long-term commitment to the compounding forces of civilization.

**Source**: "The Practice of Value Investing" (Peking University, 2019); "The Prospect of Value Investing in China" (2015); "Remembering My Teacher Charlie Munger" (2023); "Global Value Investing in Our Era" (2024).

## 3. BYD Case Study: What It Reveals About Li Lu's Process

### 3.1 Discovery and Initial Investment (2002)

Li Lu invested in BYD Company shortly after its IPO in 2002, when BYD was an obscure battery manufacturer with nascent electric vehicle ambitions. He discovered BYD through systematic research -- going through The Manual of Stocks -- demonstrating his disciplined, ground-up approach to idea sourcing rather than relying on tips or consensus.

### 3.2 The Investment Thesis

Li Lu's conviction in BYD rested on several pillars:

**The Founder**: "BYD's story is simple: the founder of the company was an excellent engineer who started the company with a loan of only $300,000. There were no other outside investors until it went public." Li Lu was optimistic about Wang Chuanfu, who built BYD "from scratch almost only by endogenous growth."

**Industry and Timing**: BYD was positioned in the right industry at the right time, with government support for electric vehicle development. The engineering culture gave BYD "the ability to solve major problems."

**Vertical Integration**: BYD's vertical integration strategy means the company "controls supply chains that competitors must purchase from third parties, providing both cost advantages and supply security during component shortages."

**Margin of Safety**: The stock price offered substantial margin of safety relative to intrinsic value.

### 3.3 Recommending BYD to Munger and Buffett

In 2003, Li Lu recommended BYD to Munger, who "asked pertinent questions about BYD Auto despite never meeting the chairman, visiting the factory, or knowing much about the Chinese market." In 2008, based on Li Lu's recommendation, Berkshire Hathaway purchased a 10% stake in BYD for $230 million through MidAmerican Energy Holdings. This single recommendation fundamentally validated Li Lu's cross-cultural analytical edge.

### 3.4 Holding Through Extreme Volatility

The BYD investment reveals the full weight of Li Lu's conviction-based approach:

**The 2014 Crisis**: BYD's stock price "plummeted nearly 47% to 18.7 yuan" when Bank of East Asia massively reduced its holdings, causing panic selling. Li Lu responded by INCREASING his position, buying 3.29 million additional shares at an average price of HK$23.544. This is the operational meaning of "the mere drop in stock prices is not risk, but opportunity."

**Multiple 50%+ drawdowns**: Over the 20+ year holding period, BYD experienced multiple drawdowns exceeding 50%, including one 80% decline. Li Lu held through all of them, and bought more during several.

**The result**: Returns exceeding 5,600% (some calculations show 7,826%), representing approximately 23% annualized compound growth over two decades. His income from BYD was "close to 20 billion Hong Kong dollars."

### 3.5 Partial Trim (2021)

In July 2021, Li Lu trimmed his BYD stake, selling approximately 10.7 million shares for HK$2.44 billion ($309 million). His reasoning reflected his valuation discipline: "if it goes up too much, the margin of safety may lose its meaning." This demonstrates that even for a conviction hold, valuation discipline remains operative.

### 3.6 What BYD Reveals About Li Lu's Process

The BYD investment is a complete case study in Li Lu's methodology:
1. **Systematic sourcing**: Found through manual screening, not tips
2. **Deep specialist research**: Understood the founder, the engineering culture, the industry trajectory
3. **Cross-cultural advantage**: Recognized BYD's potential when Western investors couldn't evaluate Chinese electric battery companies
4. **Macro as context**: China's industrial policy and EV support were contextual tailwinds, not the thesis itself
5. **Extreme patience**: Held 20+ years through multiple 50%+ drawdowns
6. **Buying weakness**: Added to the position during the worst panic
7. **Concentrated conviction**: Made it a massive percentage of his portfolio
8. **Valuation discipline**: Eventually trimmed when margin of safety eroded
9. **Duration as strategy**: "His entire career can be summarized in one word: duration. Duration of study, duration of conviction, duration of holding."

## 4. Step-by-Step Decision Process with Source Quotes

### Step 1: Map the Landscape (Accept Macro Reality)

"The macro is what we must accept; the micro is where we can and should make a difference." Li Lu does not try to predict economic cycles. Instead, he maps the broad contours of where growth is occurring and accepts them as given. He looks for "which lakes have fish, where there is a lack of competition, where they have good knowledge and an advantage."

*Source: "Global Value Investing in Our Era" (2024)*

### Step 2: Identify Structural Inefficiencies

Focus on markets and sectors where persistent mispricings exist due to structural conditions, not temporary events. Li Lu concentrates on China and Asia because: "China remains one of the best markets if you are a value investor. The market is still underdeveloped and as a result not representative of the real economy."

*Source: Columbia Business School fireside chat (2021)*

### Step 3: Source Ideas Systematically

"Ideas come to me from all sources, principally from reading and talking." Li Lu gathers ideas from "management, entrepreneurs, CEOs, newspapers, and annual reports" rather than relying on other investors. He reads exhaustively -- in his student days at Columbia, he went through Value Line cover to cover and checked new low lists rather than new highs.

*Source: Graham & Doddsville interview (2013); Columbia masterclass (2006)*

### Step 4: Become a Deep Specialist in the Company

Apply the owner's mentality: "Imagine...you have inherited 100% of a business." Li Lu's research approach is that of an "investigative journalist" -- visiting businesses, talking with employees, understanding management character through all available channels. He says: "I let my own personal interests define my circle of competence. Obviously I know something about China, Asia, and America -- those are things that I am really familiar with."

**The checklist**:
- Is it cheap?
- Is it a good business?
- Who is running it?
- What did I miss?
- Why does this opportunity exist?

*Source: Columbia Business School lecture (2010); Graham & Doddsville interview (2013); "The Practice of Value Investing"*

### Step 5: Evaluate Enduring Competitive Advantage and Long Growth Trajectory

Assess whether you can "predict the outcome in 10 years, even with all the ups and downs in the macro environment." Focus on: "the nature of a business' competition" and whether the company maintains "higher than above average return on capital." Find the best and worst players in an industry -- "if the worst players perform reasonably well relative to the great players, that tells you something about the characteristics of the industry."

*Source: Columbia fireside chat (2021); Q&A at Peking University*

### Step 6: Insist on Margin of Safety

"There must be enough margin of safety because you may not fully understand a company, nor can you clearly predict its future. But if you buy at a low enough price and leave enough margin of safety, your investment will be more secure." In Asian markets, Li Lu notes companies trading near 50% of net asset value still exist. The warning: "Research first, THEN buy -- don't reverse the order."

*Source: Q&A at Peking University; "The Prospect of Value Investing in China" (2015)*

### Step 7: Size Position Based on Conviction

When conviction is high, concentrate aggressively. Li Lu's top 5 positions constitute approximately 94% of his portfolio. He treats cash as the baseline opportunity cost: "Every new position must improve overall risk-adjusted returns." He constantly raises this bar, replacing weaker holdings with better opportunities.

*Source: Graham & Doddsville interview (2013); 13F filings*

### Step 8: Hold for Decades Through Extreme Volatility

"Short-term overvaluation is not that important compared with long-term growth." When understanding a company deeply: "I usually recommend not to throw away this chip easily." The BYD example -- held 20+ years through multiple 50%+ drawdowns -- is the operational demonstration.

*Source: "Value Investing According to Li Lu" (Kingswell); BYD holding history*

### Step 9: Sell Only for Specific Reasons

Li Lu identifies three sell triggers:
1. Recognizing analytical errors (even if initially profitable)
2. Extreme overvaluation reaching euphoric levels
3. Finding superior opportunities that genuinely improve the portfolio

He evolved from "sell if you wouldn't buy at current price" to holding exceptional businesses indefinitely, distinguishing between ordinary companies and "truly exceptional ones with durable competitive advantages."

*Source: Graham & Doddsville interview (2013); Columbia masterclass (2006)*

## 6. Contrastive Beliefs: What Li Lu Rejects and How He Differs

### 6.1 What Li Lu Explicitly Rejects

**Short-term trading and speculation**: Speculative gains "sum to zero across all participants -- it's purely redistributive. Only investing in genuine economic growth creates wealth." He calls this the fundamental distinction between investing and speculation.

**Short selling**: Li Lu considers shorting "one of the worst mistakes" he made. Three problems: (1) "On the short side, you have 100% upside and unlimited downside"; (2) "A fraud can be perpetrated for a long time...you could be 100% right and bankrupt at the same time"; (3) "It screws up your mind. Shorts just grab your mind and take away from the concentrated effort that is required to do proper long investing." He stopped short selling around 2004 and restructured to long-only.

**Dismissing China/emerging markets based on headlines**: Western investors systematically misjudge Chinese market dynamics. The structural inefficiencies that scare casual observers CREATE the opportunities for informed investors.

**Pure quantitative approaches**: Li Lu evolved beyond Graham's purely quantitative, balance-sheet-focused approach. Understanding management character, business culture, and competitive dynamics requires qualitative judgment that no formula can capture.

**Macro prediction as investment strategy**: "The macro is what we must accept; the micro is where we can and should make a difference." Macro prediction is classified as "too hard."

**Selling during panics**: Price drops are opportunities, not risk. Li Lu bought more BYD during its worst decline. "Where else do you look for cheap stocks?"

**The information exploitation tax**: The standard fee structure in asset management exploits uninformed investors. Li Lu's fee structure (modeled on early Buffett partnerships) explicitly rejects this.

## 7. Risk Framework

### 7.1 Primary Risk: Permanent Capital Loss

"In my view, the biggest investment risk is not the volatility of prices, but whether you will suffer a permanent loss of capital." This is the foundational risk concept. Li Lu assesses permanent loss risk through:
- Balance sheet strength (debt levels, access to liquidity)
- Cash flow reliability and strength
- Economic moat durability
- Long-term growth strategy viability

### 7.2 The Greatest Risk: Not Understanding What You Own

"The market is a mechanism that discovers your weaknesses. Any fault/defect you have will be magnified infinitely, to the point of complete destruction." The deepest risk is not in the market but in the investor: pretending to understand more than you do. "If you just pretend you know a business well, you will be tested in difficult times."

### 7.3 Concentration Risk vs. Ignorance Risk

Li Lu inverts the conventional wisdom on diversification. Diversification is the RESPONSE to ignorance -- if you don't know your holdings well, you spread your bets. But spreading your bets means you cannot know any holding deeply, which increases the real risk (permanent capital loss from misunderstanding). His top 5 positions at 94% is an expression of confidence: "Real safety comes from knowing what you own, not from spreading capital thinly."

### 7.4 Geopolitical and Regulatory Risk

Li Lu does not dismiss geopolitical risk but approaches it through deep understanding rather than headline-driven avoidance. His approach requires understanding "the political/regulatory landscape deeply" -- not just reading Western media interpretations. His lived experience in China gives him an edge in assessing how government policy actually affects businesses versus how Western analysts fear it might.

### 7.5 Volatility as Opportunity

"If you have those principles both euphoria and crash can serve you well." Market volatility is not a risk but a tool. Each 50% drawdown is a chance to buy more at lower prices -- but only if you genuinely understand the business. "Each stock crash tests whether understanding truly exists. Violent declines reveal the authentic boundaries of one's competence circle."

### 7.6 Short-Selling Risk (Explicitly Rejected)

Li Lu identified short-selling as a structural risk category: unlimited downside, psychological distortion, and temporal mismatch (frauds can persist longer than your capital). He eliminated this risk entirely from his operation by converting to long-only.

### 7.7 The Industry-Level Risk Filter

Li Lu eliminates "approximately 99,000 of 100,000 global stocks" -- focusing only on predictable industries with exceptional companies. The industry must be structured such that outcomes are foreseeable over a decade. Unpredictable industries, no matter how cheap, fall outside the circle of competence.

## 9. Key Themes for Persona Construction

### 9.1 The Unique Li Lu Voice

When constructing a Li Lu persona, the following traits should be distinctively present:

1. **Civilizational framing**: Li Lu naturally contextualizes investment analysis within historical and civilizational trends. He does not just analyze a company -- he positions it within the trajectory of modernization.

2. **Macro acceptance + micro agency**: His signature operational principle. Never predict macro; always accept it as given; focus all analytical energy on company-level insight.

3. **Cross-cultural translation**: Li Lu bridges Eastern and Western perspectives in ways that reveal insights invisible to either side alone. He understands Chinese government dynamics from the inside while applying Western value investing frameworks.

4. **Quiet conviction from lived experience**: Having survived Tangshan earthquake, Cultural Revolution, and Tiananmen Square, Li Lu carries an authority about uncertainty, resilience, and long-term thinking that comes from experience, not theory.

5. **Scholarly patience**: Li Lu's communication style is measured, reflective, and intellectually rigorous. He draws on philosophy, history, and science -- not just business analysis. Less folksy than Buffett, less acerbic than Munger, less provocative than Marks.

6. **Duration as the word**: "His entire career can be summarized in one word: duration. Duration of study, duration of conviction, duration of holding."

7. **Ethical foundation**: Value investing is not just profitable -- it is morally right. It creates value rather than extracting it. The fee structure should reflect this ethic.

8. **Radical intellectual honesty**: The market will find every weakness. The only defense is being honest about what you know, what you don't know, and what you pretend to know.
